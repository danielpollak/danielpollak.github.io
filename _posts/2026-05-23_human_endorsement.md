To whom it may concern, I am writing to enthusiastically endorse being human for admission into the Neurobiology and CNS options of the Department of Biology and Biological Engineering at Caltech. Being human has established itself as having an excellent reputation over the course of hundreds of thousands of years, and in many other departments at Caltech, by establishing 1) frequent, 2) meaningful, and 3) diverse interactions between colleagues. Here is why I believe NB and CNS would benefit from being human. 

### Being human is more than going to work.

Between siloing ourselves away to "be scientists", and [falling over ourselves](!https://markusmeister.com/2025/09/15/what-human-intelligence-are-you-talking-about/) to make the grandest offering to the LLMs, in the NB and CNS options, we have stopped engaging in the intermediate activity of trying to be more human. 

### Being more human is bumping up against a lot of other humans.

In my six years here, the opportunities to bump up against the <100 people who also do neuroscience have been few and far between. There are people in my department at Caltech who I have literally only ever heard of. And Caltech's BBE department is pitifully small compared to a real university's. And I don't just mean bumping up against your labmates, though that would be a good start. 

One of the things people love about Caltech is how many smart people work there. One of the virtues of working at Caltech is that your smartness gets to be multiplied by the smartness of all the people around you. You get to cross-pollinate, meet friends, colleagues, collaborators, partners. Unless no one talks to people outside of their lab. Unless the intention of the place you work is to minimize time spent chatting and learning things about each other. The closest I've ever felt to many smart people here is from reading their twitter.

This is a heavy accusation to levy against an institution. It's hard to prove at an institutional level that there is a conspiracy to isolate people from each other. But you can make some limited conclusions based on what an institution, like BBE, does NOT do. BBE does NOT (in contrast with every other department at Caltech as far as I'm aware) do something considered to be so low-stakes that it is non-negotiable at every other prestigious instution.

It does not have a weekly beer hour.

Earlier I put "being scientists" in quotation marks. "Doing science" does not start or end with collecting data or writing a paper. It starts and ends with looking someone in the eyes and telling them about it. I also outlined the three criteria for being human: having frequent, meaningful, and diverse interactions with people. Let's talk about it.

### Being more human requires frequent interactions
I just got back from the Chen Institute's [retreat](!https://www.caltech.edu/campus-life-events/calendar/chen-institute-retreat-2) to Lake Arrowhead, which took place from the 15th to the 17th of May. I met people there who had been at Caltech for years for the first time. Not only have they been at Caltech for years, some even worked in my building. Others I saw for the first time in weeks or months. It was a lovely time. I jumped into the lake, played tennis against one of the greats in the field of machine vision, and shared meals with my friends. 

Fantastic. But I got the sense that the Chen Institute thinks this is enough. There's no Chen Institute-funded weekly beer hour, nor is there any weekly event that I have access to (the "tech-free tea" on Wednesdays is timed to overlap with our weekly lab meetings). One retreat once every other year ain't gonna cut the mustard on this one. 

My friends and I have valiantly fought against this rising tide of isolation in BBE by hosting Neurotecher Happy Hours, of which there have been exactly five in the last year. That corresponds to roughly one happy hour every quarter. Let's take a taste... yep, WHEW! the mustard is still too spicy... Once a quarter ain't gonna cut it neither.

Humans can barely get through a week without interacting socially. Why are we making them wait months to see their colleagues? 

I admit it's weird to lament the social connections between coworkers. If we weren't in academia, I'd agree it's just a job. For many, it is just a job, and that's fine. But for the brief moment where I have the privilege of being a graduate student, I, and most everyone else I've ever talked to at Caltech, would like to be able to bump into people more often. If our job weren't to discover and share the nature of the universe with the world, I'd agree that it's weird to lament the social connections between coworkers. But it is our job, so we better fucking chat.

### Being more human requires meaningful interactions
I walk past this one guy on the way to the bathroom every day. I don't know his name and he doesn't know mine because even though this is a bona fide bumping into someone, we both have to shit. All bumps are not made equal.

Historically, beverages, (alcoholic)[!https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2021/07/america-drinking-alone-problem/619017/] and not, have been pretty important for human cohesion. That's why universities have so universally settled on beer hours. It's a strategic advantage, and at $100-200/week, I sense it has a pretty good ROI. I have no real data to back this up and I will not look for any. It's just a fact that drinking things with other people makes you closer to them.

We used to have a monthly beer hour because I said all this to the provost at some point. It was lovely, until someone complained that someone came back to lab drunk, and then it was cancelled.

### Being human requires diverse interactions
Again I see my labmates every week, sometimes even every day. I'd go crazy without them. But it's important to meet new people too, and maintain the spectrum of relationships we have with people across the institute. This is self evident. If this is not self evident to you, I heartily recommend retiring.

### There's more social interaction on the Big Bang Theory than at actual Caltech
In my early days at Caltech I was surprised by how true the Big Bang Theory was to its inspiration, Caltech, in terms of the personality profile it attracted. I realize now I have been unkind to the Big Bang Theory. All those hopeless nerds do is hang out with each other. This caracature of an imagined paragon of awkward socially inadequate Caltech geeks is currently casting the denizens of neuroscience at Caltech is too positive a light. 

Caltech, BBE, Tianqiao, whoever, just give the neuroscientists a beer hour. Make some grad student host it every week. Make it a requirment for graduation. Allocate the funding. And whoever complained that someone came to lab drunk, talk to the drunken scientist, but don't collectively punish everyone else trying to enjoy themselves at the end of a hard week.
